Case study — 03 / Personal data

One year of seat time, one chart.

115 laps at Thunderhill West across five track days, logged on two apps that disagree with each other. The interesting part wasn't building the chart. It was redesigning it a year later and finding what I'd let slide because I already knew the answer.

01 — The rebuild

Every lap, in order, with the floor underneath

Each mark is one lap. The heavy line under each track day is that day's theoretical best — my fastest sectors, strung together. The floor: pace I'd already proven in pieces but hadn't put in one lap.

The gap between floor and cluster is the honest measure. Not "am I faster?" — that flatters you, because tyres and weather and traffic all move. It's "how close am I to the lap I've already proven?"

02 — What I changed

Reviewing my own chart a year later

The original is still on Tableau Public. A serviceable chart; I was pleased with it. Coming back cold, I could see where I'd designed for someone who already knew the answer — which is to say, for me.

Legend

Two swatches, no words

A teal dot and a purple dot, unlabelled. I knew which was which. Nobody else did. Now they say dry and wet.

Overplotting

Marks the size of the story

Large opaque circles buried the early sessions in one blob. Smaller marks with some transparency let density read as density.

Annotation

Leader lines across the data

Callouts drew rules straight through the plot. Detail now sits in a readout below, so nothing is drawn over the data.

Axis

Seconds nobody thinks in

It read 95s, 100s, 105s. Nobody at a track day talks that way. Now it's m:ss, and the x-axis is split by track day instead of an unlabelled run.

None of it is dramatic. That's the point — it's the ordinary maintenance a chart needs when it stops being something you made and becomes something someone reads. I only caught it by treating my own work as though someone else submitted it.

03 — What the data said

Eleven seconds, and where they came from

1:39 to 1:28.39 — eleven seconds over a season, almost none of it in one leap. The theoretical-best floor drops steadily, and the cluster follows at a lag. I could always drive the sectors before I could drive the lap.

The wet session is the most useful thing here, and I nearly filtered it out for being ugly. Forty seconds of penalty on the same day I set my best time argues about context better than any dry lap could. The outlier wasn't noise. It was the finding.
