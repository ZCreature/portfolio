Case study - 05 / Competition

Burnout as a heat map

A study of medical student mental health, measured on two validated instruments and read as density instead of as points.

Built for the Cleartelligence Chart Champ competition, where it placed fourth. The top five entries did not just get judged, they got defended. I presented this from a stage at Boston University, to a room, in one pass. Designing for that is a different job from designing for a reader who can lean in.

01 - Context

An entry for Chart Champ 2023

Cleartelligence ran its eighth annual Chart Champ contest on 4 October 2023 at Boston University. The theme that year was higher education, dashboards were due 22 September, and the five highest-placed entries were presented live at the event.

The subject I picked under that theme is medical student mental health: survey scores for depression, burnout and exhaustion, set against curriculum year and weekly study hours. The measures correspond to a public dataset whose fields include cesd, stud_h, health and the MBI subscales.

02 - Process

Two panels, because two questions

The data offered more than two questions and the canvas offered room for two. Depression severity against curriculum year went left. Weekly study hours, the variable a student can actually change, went right, carrying three stacked measures over one shared x axis.

Density marks were the second decision. A few hundred students plotted as points is a smear, and a binned heat map introduces cell edges that read as structure the data does not have. Tableau's density marks are kernel-smoothed, so repeated responses at the same coordinates accumulate into continuous regions with a readable center.

Built in Tableau and published two days ahead of the deadline, with one revision before the event.

03 - Design

Density marks across two panels

The canvas is 1,366 by 795. On the left, severity of depression symptoms runs 0 to 60 against curriculum year 1 to 6, on a grey-to-teal ramp. On the right, three strips stack over a shared x axis of hours of study per week, 0 to 75: academic burnout at 0 to 40, emotional exhaustion at 0 to 35, and mental health at 1 to 6, all on a grey-to-orange-to-red ramp.

A pale grey silhouette of a head in profile occupies the right quarter of the canvas and carries no data. Axis titles and tick labels are the only text on the dashboard.

04 - Impact

Impact

The dashboard placed fourth overall. Because the top five were presented live, the work did not end at submission. I stood up in front of the room at Boston University and defended it out loud.

That changes what a chart has to do. A dashboard read alone gets as long as the reader wants. A dashboard read from a stage gets one pass, at the speaker's pace, from the back of the room. The two-panel split is what survived that constraint. Depression severity against curriculum year on the left, three strips over weekly study hours on the right, and the whole argument reduces to a shape people can see before I finish the sentence describing it: the load does not taper as students advance.

Density marks earned their place for the same reason. A scatter of a few hundred students is a smear from twenty feet away. Kernel-smoothed density turns repeated responses into a region with a readable center, so the pattern holds at presentation distance instead of dissolving into ink.

What I would change is the thing the room could have asked about and did not. The published dashboard carries no title, legend, units note or source line, because I was standing next to it supplying all of that. Once it was back on Tableau Public without me, that context was gone. A chart that has to travel without its author needs to answer those questions on its own face.
