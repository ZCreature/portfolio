Case study — 01 / Design systems & accessibility

Defining the colour standard for healthcare data visualization.

Fifty-plus executive dashboards for U.S. hospital systems. The team needed one answer to "is this chart readable?" I led the study that produced it, then built the Figma system that made it the default.

Role

Sr. Designer, Data Intelligence & Visualization

Client

Vizient — U.S. hospital systems

Years

2022 — present

Tools

NDA

Client data can't be shown publicly. This page is the method and the system — the rules, the reasoning, and charts rebuilt from synthetic data that obey them. Real screens on request.

01 — The problem

Fifty dashboards, fifty different answers

Executives read these charts to make decisions with real consequences — often through a boardroom projector's washed-out colour, sometimes printed to greyscale, occasionally on a phone.

Accessibility was handled per-dashboard, by whoever designed it. Everyone made reasonable choices. Collectively there was no standard — and no definition of what "compliant" even meant for a chart. WCAG is written for text and UI components, not for eleven categorical series on a stacked bar.

WCAG tells you a label needs 4.5:1. It doesn't tell you how many series a colourblind reader can tell apart.

02 — The study

What I actually tested

I audited the dashboards already shipping, not a hypothetical. Three questions, in order:

  • Do labels pass at every tint? Brand colour ships at 60%, 30%, 10%. Contrast has to hold at every step, not just full strength.
  • Do series survive colour-vision deficiency? All three types, at partial severity as well as full — most colourblind readers aren't at 100%.
  • Does it survive with no colour at all? Greyscale print and projector washout are the same failure as CVD: colour stops carrying information.
4.5:1

Text on any fill

AA for body-size labels, checked at every tint step rather than at 100% only.

3:1

Adjacent fills

Non-text contrast between neighbouring segments, so a boundary reads without a stroke.

ΔE 10

Series separation

Minimum perceptual distance between any two series after CVD simulation.

5

Categorical ceiling

Past five categorical series, colour stops working. Group, facet, or use position instead.

Where the ceiling of five comes from

I'd been repeating "six" out of habit, so I checked. I solved for the best achievable palette at each size and measured the worst pair under every viewing condition at once.

03 — The standard, tested

A chart that holds up

A chart built to the standard, from synthetic data. The controls run the same simulation the study used. This should be boring — nothing should collapse.

04 — The system

Making the standard the path of least resistance

A standard nobody follows is a document. This one stuck because it went into the Figma library the team already used: verified palettes as styles, chart components with tints pre-applied, annotation patterns for where colour wasn't enough.

Designers stopped picking colours per chart. They picked a component and compliance came with it. The accessible option became the fastest option — the only version that survives a deadline.

What it didn't solve

Legacy dashboards didn't retro-fit themselves. And the ΔE 10 series floor is my threshold, not a standards body's — I'd defend it, but it's a judgement call and I flag it as one.

Checking the rules by hand was still slow. That led to the next project.
